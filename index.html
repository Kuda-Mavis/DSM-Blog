Maternal Health in India

Regional Inequalities and the Role of Healthcare Access

Kudakwashe Mavis Chakanyuka

26 April 2026

Introduction

Maternal health remains a critical concern in many parts of the world, particularly in developing countries where disparities in healthcare access and socioeconomic conditions significantly influence outcomes. Despite improvements in medical infrastructure and policy interventions, maternal mortality and inadequate access to essential maternal healthcare services continue to pose serious challenges. Key factors such as availability of skilled healthcare personnel, institutional delivery services, antenatal care, and broader socioeconomic conditions play a crucial role in determining maternal health outcomes, and their combined influence varies significantly across regions, countries, and communities.

This project examines how differences in healthcare access and socioeconomic conditions are associated with variations in maternal health outcomes across states in India. Using state-level data on institutional delivery rates, antenatal care coverage, financial access, anaemia prevalence, fertility indicators, and infant mortality rate (IMR), the study aims to identify patterns and regional disparities. The objective is not to establish causation, but to explore relationships between these indicators and understand how variations in access and social conditions correspond to differences in maternal health outcomes across diverse population groups, settings, and contexts, over time periods.

How much does access shape maternal health outcomes across Indian states?

Problem Statement

Maternal health outcomes are shaped by a combination of healthcare access, socioeconomic conditions, and demographic factors. While access to services has increased across India, the benefits are not evenly distributed, and some regions continue to experience poorer outcomes. These differences are influenced by factors such as healthcare availability, education levels, socioeconomic conditions, and regional inequalities in infrastructure and resources. The core problem is not just limited access, but a lack of understanding of how these factors interact to shape maternal health outcomes across regions. Using state-level indicators such as institutional delivery, antenatal care, financial access, and infant mortality rates, this study examines how these factors are associated with variations in outcomes across states. Without understanding these relationships, it becomes difficult to identify where interventions are most needed and how resources can be effectively allocated to reduce disparities and improve outcomes.

Data and Methodology

This project applies concepts from data science and database systems to study maternal health outcomes. Data is collected from multiple sources using APIs and combined using common fields such as state name, allowing datasets to be connected and analysed together. The analysis uses exploratory data analysis, correlation analysis, and regression modelling to identify patterns and quantify relationships between healthcare access indicators and infant mortality rate. The findings aim to support data-driven insights that can inform policy decisions and targeted public health interventions.

Data Sources and Preparation

The primary datasets are sourced from the NDAP API and include the National Family Health Survey (NFHS) providing state-level indicators on institutional delivery, antenatal care, financial access, anaemia, and adolescent fertility; the Sample Registration System (SRS) providing healthcare access indicators; NSSO and SRS mortality data providing infant mortality rate (IMR) as the primary outcome variable; and state-level fertility indicators providing demographic context. These datasets are integrated to enable a comprehensive, multi-dimensional analysis that captures both healthcare system factors and underlying demographic variations influencing maternal and child health outcomes.

Data cleaning was carried out using Python. The NFHS dataset was filtered to retain only relevant indicators, column names were standardised, and state names were cleaned to enable accurate merging. The mortality dataset was filtered to extract IMR, with multiple entries per state aggregated by mean. Missing values were removed where key variables were unavailable, and duplicate entries were eliminated. The cleaned datasets were merged on state name, producing a unified dataset of 46 state-level observations with 9 variables suitable for analysis. Additional validation checks were performed to ensure consistency across variables and confirm the integrity of the merged dataset before proceeding to analysis.

Analytical Approach

The cleaned dataset was stored in a relational SQLite database, organised as a single table where each row represents a unique state and residence category. SQL queries were used to extract aggregated insights, compare indicators across rural and urban populations, and identify states with the highest and lowest outcomes. This structure allowed for straightforward querying and comparison without requiring complex joins. The single-table design reflected the aggregated nature of the dataset, where each observation represents a unique combination of state and residence type, making it well-suited to the scope and scale of this analysis. Indexes were also applied to key fields such as state and residence category to improve query efficiency and performance.

To formally examine relationships between variables, Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated to assess the strength and direction of linear associations between healthcare indicators and IMR. A multiple linear regression model was then developed to quantify these relationships, using institutional delivery, antenatal care, and adolescent fertility as explanatory variables. Coefficients were estimated using Ordinary Least Squares (OLS), which minimises the sum of squared residuals to produce the best linear fit to the observed data. All findings are interpreted as associations rather than causal relationships, given the cross-sectional nature of the dataset. Consequently, the analysis does not capture temporal dynamics or fully control for potential confounding factors that may influence the observed relationships.

Exploratory Data Analysis

The final merged dataset consists of 46 observations and 9 variables representing state-level indicators across rural and urban populations. Initial inspection shows no missing values, indicating the data is complete and suitable for analysis. Summary statistics reveal variation across states in key indicators such as institutional delivery rates, antenatal care coverage, and IMR, confirming the presence of regional differences. The dataset covers 23 states across both rural and urban residence categories, providing a broad geographic basis for comparison. Descriptive statistics show that IMR ranges from a low of 4.22 in Kerala Urban to a high of 29.22 in Madhya Pradesh Rural, illustrating the scale of inequality present in the data. This wide range highlights substantial disparities in healthcare access and outcomes between different regions and residence types, reflecting underlying differences in infrastructure, resource allocation, and socioeconomic conditions that influence maternal and child health.

Institutional delivery rates exhibit a clear left-skewed distribution, with most values concentrated between 85% and 100%, indicating that a large number of states have high levels of institutional healthcare access while only a few lag behind. Antenatal care coverage displays a moderately symmetric distribution with most observations clustered between 60% and 80%. Adolescent fertility exhibits a right-skewed distribution with most states clustered at lower values and a long tail extending toward higher levels, indicating significant demographic disparities. Infant mortality rate shows a mildly right-skewed distribution with most values concentrated between 10 and 20, while a few states extend toward 30, suggesting that while many states experience moderate mortality levels, some continue to face relatively higher risks.

Correlation Analysis

Correlation analysis indicates that healthcare access variables are negatively associated with IMR. Institutional delivery and antenatal care both show moderate negative correlations, suggesting that higher access to formal healthcare services is linked to better maternal and child health outcomes. The relationship between institutional delivery and IMR shows a clear negative trend, where states with higher proportions of institutional births tend to exhibit lower infant mortality rates. A similar negative association is observed for antenatal care coverage, reinforcing the importance of prenatal healthcare. These patterns are consistent with broader public health evidence highlighting the role of timely and accessible maternal care in reducing infant mortality risks.

In contrast, financial access shows little to no clear relationship with IMR. The regression line appears relatively flat, and the clustering of observations at higher financial access levels, alongside the presence of outliers, suggests that financial inclusion alone does not strongly explain variation in infant mortality outcomes. Adolescent fertility demonstrates a positive relationship with IMR, where states with higher adolescent fertility rates tend to experience higher infant mortality levels, highlighting it as a key demographic risk factor. The noticeable dispersion of points around regression lines indicates that these relationships, while meaningful, are not strongly deterministic, and that additional socioeconomic and regional factors also play a role.

Regression Analysis

A multiple linear regression model was developed to quantify the relationship between healthcare access indicators and infant mortality rate. The estimated model is :

IMR = 44.57 − 0.253 (Institutional Delivery) − 0.095 (Antenatal Care) + 0.025 (Adolescent Fertility)

The coefficients confirm the expected theoretical directions. Institutional delivery has the strongest effect, where a one percentage point increase is associated with an approximate decrease of 0.25 units in IMR. A one percentage point increase in antenatal care is associated with a smaller decrease of approximately 0.09 units in IMR. In contrast, a one unit increase in adolescent fertility is associated with an increase of approximately 0.02 units in IMR. These results suggest that improvements in institutional delivery have the most substantial impact on reducing infant mortality among the variables considered.

Model Evaluation

The model achieves an R² of 0.30, meaning it explains approximately 30% of the variation in IMR across states. A comparison of actual and predicted IMR values shows that the model captures the general trends in the data, although some variation remains unexplained. Residual analysis highlights that prediction errors vary across observations, suggesting the presence of unobserved socioeconomic or regional factors that also influence maternal and child health outcomes. This indicates that additional variables could improve the model’s explanatory power and provide a more comprehensive understanding of the determinants of IMR.

For a practical illustration, a hypothetical scenario with institutional delivery of 90%, antenatal care coverage of 75%, and adolescent fertility of 30 yields an estimated IMR of approximately 15.37. The model is based on cross-sectional data from a single year and therefore captures associations rather than causal relationships or temporal trends. Results should be interpreted as indicative rather than predictive of future outcomes and the estimates are best understood as illustrative of potential patterns under similar conditions rather than precise forecasts of real-world outcomes, serving to demonstrate how variations in key indicators may be associated with changes in IMR within the scope of the model.

Key Insights

The analysis identifies several consistent patterns across the data. States with higher institutional delivery rates and stronger antenatal care coverage reliably exhibit lower infant mortality rates, confirming that access to formal healthcare services is a key determinant of maternal and child health outcomes. Rural areas consistently show lower healthcare access and higher IMR compared to urban areas, reflecting structural inequalities in the distribution of healthcare resources. Adolescent fertility emerges as a significant demographic risk factor, with higher rates associated with increased infant mortality across both rural and urban populations. Together, these findings highlight the interconnected role of healthcare access and demographic factors in shaping regional disparities in maternal and child health outcomes.

Financial access, while broadly important, does not independently explain variation in IMR, suggesting that financial inclusion must be complemented by effective healthcare infrastructure and service delivery to translate into improved outcomes. States like Kerala and Tamil Nadu demonstrate what is achievable with sustained investment, while states such as Madhya Pradesh and Uttar Pradesh highlight where the most urgent gaps remain. Collectively, the findings point to a need for targeted, multi-dimensional interventions that address both healthcare access and the underlying demographic and socioeconomic conditions that shape outcomes, with a focus on reducing regional disparities.

Limitations

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings of this study. The dataset is cross-sectional, covering a single year, which means it captures associations at a point in time rather than changes over time. This limits the ability to draw conclusions about trends or causal relationships. The regression model explains approximately 30% of the variation in IMR, indicating that a substantial portion of the differences across states is driven by factors not captured in the model, such as quality of healthcare services, geographic accessibility, cultural factors, and broader governance.

The financial access variable showed data inconsistencies, including extreme negative outliers, which may affect the reliability of findings related to that indicator. Additionally, the dataset aggregates observations at the state level, which may mask within-state variation across districts or communities. All findings should therefore be interpreted as indicative of associations rather than definitive causal effects, and used to inform rather than replace more detailed policy analysis. Further research using more granular, longitudinal data could help validate these patterns and provide deeper insights into the underlying dynamics, across different regions and population groups.

Conclusion

This study set out to understand how healthcare access shapes maternal and child health outcomes across Indian states. The data consistently shows that states with higher institutional delivery rates and stronger antenatal care coverage tend to have lower infant mortality, while high adolescent fertility remains a persistent risk factor. These patterns hold across both rural and urban populations, though the scale of the gaps between them points to structural inequalities that extend beyond healthcare access alone. States like Kerala and Tamil Nadu demonstrate that sustained investment in maternal health infrastructure can produce outcomes that stand apart from the national average, offering a reference point for what is achievable. At the other end of the spectrum, states such as Madhya Pradesh and Uttar Pradesh continue to face significantly higher infant mortality rates, underscoring how much ground remains to be covered.

The regression analysis and visualisations reinforce what these patterns suggest: improving access to safe childbirth and prenatal care is consistently associated with better outcomes, but no single indicator fully explains variation in infant mortality. Healthcare access, demographic factors such as adolescent fertility, and broader socioeconomic conditions interact in ways that shape outcomes across regions. What the data makes clear is that progress is possible, but uneven, and closely tied to how resources and opportunities are distributed across states. Addressing maternal and child health at scale will therefore require not just expanding services, but ensuring that institutional delivery and antenatal care improvements reach populations that remain underserved. Ultimately, the analysis highlights that maternal health is as much an issue of equitable access as it is of healthcare provision, and closing these gaps will demand sustained, targeted commitment from policymakers, with disparities in outcomes reflecting deeper structural inequalities across regions.
